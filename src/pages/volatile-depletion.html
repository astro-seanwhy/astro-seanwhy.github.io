Volatile Depletion and the Star–Planet Chemical Connection

During my PhD at the Australian National University, I developed the first empirical model of devolatilization, quantifying how volatile elements are depleted in planetary materials relative to their host stars, a process long recognized but never systematically measured. This work demonstrated that Earth and the Sun share nearly identical refractory compositions but diverge in volatiles due to planet formation, establishing a quantitative link between stellar and planetary compositions. It has since inspired studies underpinning devolatilization as a universal process in rocky-planet formation, including evidence from polluted white dwarfs and co-natal stellar pairs.

At ETH Zürich, I extended this fiducial model to exoplanetary systems by integrating stellar abundance measurements, Solar System chemistry, and exoplanet mass-radius data. This work established a predictive connection between stellar composition and planetary bulk properties and broadened the scope to include chemical diversity among host stars, advancing studies on condensation chemistry and volatility variations across different stellar environments.

As Assistant Professor at the University of Copenhagen, I have mainly developed a physical model of devolatilization during pebble accretion, coupled with planetesimal formation and collisional assembly in a Bayesian framework. This model explains the bulk compositions of terrestrial planets and, by extension, terrestrial-type exoplanets through hybrid accretion (Wang et al. 2026, Nature Astronomy), and represents the first attempt to embed devolatilization physics directly into planet-formation theory.
